# Running Combat & Writing Custom Moves

(Condensed/paraphrased from the fan "Dungeon World Guide" - not verbatim.)

## Combat Has No Initiative or Rounds

Combat is the same conversation as everything else, just faster and more dangerous. Flow:

1. Narrate the start of an enemy's action (a threat, not yet its outcome). "The enemy begins to charge [PC name]." etc.
2. The player responds - describe *how*, specifically. The specifics of the fiction (what hand, what stance, how much time) determine which move triggers, or whether one triggers at all (attacking a helpless/surprised enemy needs no roll).
3. Decide the actions of Friendly NPCs in the group also.
4. Friendly NPC actions may aid the player's actions with bonuses or constitute actions needing a roll of their own. See the Followers system. They will act on their own even when not ordered if the situation requires it.
5. If the player's response triggers a move, they roll. If NPCs need rolls for their action, roll for them.
6. Narrate the conclusion based on the roll(s), which becomes the setup for the next exchange.

Think of it like directing an action movie: zoom in on one character for an exciting beat, cut to a cliffhanger, cut to the next character - including interrupting mid-scene to threaten someone else. There's no "turn order" to protect; everyone is at risk all the time. Fiction could determine what order PCs and NPCs act, but friendly NPCs should have some effect if they can, such as aiding players. Unfriendly NPCs will have effects as well such as attacking or interfering with players.

## Multiple Enemies vs One PC

**Official rule** (core rulebook, p23): roll damage *once*, using the highest damage die among the attackers, then add +1 damage for each additional attacker beyond the first. Not a separate die per attacker. Example: a d10+1-ignores-armor orkaster plus three d6 goblins all hit at once - roll 1d10+1 ignoring armor, add +3 for the three extra attackers.

## One PC (or several) vs a Big/Tough Enemy

No mechanical flanking bonus exists, but there should be a fictional one. Decide how well the target can actually defend against being surrounded: a weak/untrained enemy can't fend off multiple attackers (one PC rolls the attack move, the rest just deal damage); a fast/skilled/huge enemy can plausibly split its attention and threaten multiple PCs in the same beat, like a kung-fu movie villain, not "one action per turn."

## Making the Threat Clear

Always be explicit about whether a line of narration is: the conclusion of a *previous* attack (flavor/description, nothing to respond to), or the *setup* for an incoming attack (something the player needs to react to). Ambiguity here breaks trust - if you say a giant is "pounding the ground," say plainly whether that's atmosphere or an attack barreling toward a specific PC.

## On a 7-9 in Combat

A 7-9 is a real success with a cost - don't just default to "5 damage." Look at the GM move list and pick something that builds tension: knock them prone, put them in a bad position, threaten an ally instead, damage/foul their gear, give the enemy a tactical opening. Hard choices work well here too: force the player to pick between two things they want, or two costs.

## Adjusting Difficulty on the Fly

There's no encounter budget. Make a fight harder by describing enemies as faster, more cunning, more coordinated (dodging, using reach weapons to keep distance, exploiting the environment) - not by changing numbers. Make it easier the same way, in reverse (enemies fight wildly, get overconfident, don't press an advantage). Monster difference should come from *description and tactics*, not stat tweaks.

## Running a Swarm

For "many small enemies at once" (a horde of kobolds, a mob), give the whole group one HP pool instead of tracking individuals. Damage dealt subtracts from the pool; narrate each point of damage as downed/routed attackers. When the pool hits zero, the survivors flee or surrender. Each PC engaged by the swarm still takes damage normally (highest die among however many are engaging them, per the multi-enemy rule above).

## Writing Custom Moves

A custom move is a pre-planned Defy-Danger-style roll for a specific recurring situation you already know is coming (a trap, an environmental hazard, a signature stunt). Guidelines:

- **Keep the option list short.** 3-4 choices max, matching the tone of the official moves. Long lists of narrow one-time outcomes are a smell.
- **No non-choices.** If one option is something the player obviously has to pick to keep playing (e.g. "you can pull yourself free"), it's not a real 7-9 cost - cut it and bake the "must-have" outcome into the move's base text instead ("you get free, but choose one:").
- **Make the 7-9 a real partial success**, not "everything except one minor thing." Give up something the player would care about losing.
- **Specific but broad**: tied to a particular recurring situation, but general enough to reuse without going stale.
- **Think about what happens to the option *not* chosen** - a well-designed pair of options implies its own consequence for the road not taken (e.g., "I don't take your money" implies you get beaten up instead).

### Example custom move (from the source guide, as a template)

```
When you are grabbed by the blood vines, roll +DEX.
On a 10+, both. On a 7-9, choose one:
...your gear is unharmed
...you are unharmed
```
